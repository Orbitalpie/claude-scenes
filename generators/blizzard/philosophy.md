# Gale Grammar

An algorithmic philosophy for the blizzard backdrop.

A blizzard isn't falling snow. It's air made visible. Here the wind is written first and the snow only reads it. The scene starts from a stream function: layered value noise, drifting in scale with depth. Its curl is a velocity field with no sources or sinks. Every swirl it makes closes on itself, as real air does. A steady prevailing wind is added on top, leaning the whole field down and across. From that field, streamlines are traced step by step, hundreds of them. These are the invisible paths the snow will ride. Choosing the noise scale, the curl strength and the wind's lean so the eddies feel heavy rather than decorative takes painstaking tuning. The values should feel worked out over a long winter, not guessed.

The snow falls as weather really does: in depth. Four layers fall at their own speeds, each leaning a little with the wind and fluttering as the air shifts. The far flakes are many, tiny and slow; the near ones are few, large, soft-edged and fast. On top of that steady fall, sheets of heavier snow ride across the land on the wind. Each sheet is a band of denser flakes inside a faint haze, so you see the curtains of a real storm march past. The swirling flow field from earlier now shapes only the gusts, as near-horizontal streaks of wind-driven snow.

Intensity is a weather system, not a loop. Several layers have their own breathing cycles, each a prime number of seconds long: steady light snow, a heavier fall, streaking gust lines, and a whiteout veil. Because the cycles never line up for long, the storm keeps changing its mind. It slackens to a few drifting flakes, builds, tears through in long horizontal streaks, swallows the forest in white, then eases again. Now and then several peaks coincide and the storm is truly bad. Those rare moments are the reward of the tuning.

The land is almost gone. Pines are grown from stacked, ragged tiers whose widths follow a slightly irregular taper. Their snow-loaded tips droop, and pale caps sit on each tier. Bare birches and larches are thin recursive skeletons. Three bands of forest sink into the fog by depth, and the farthest is softly blurred, so the trees are felt more than seen. Snowdrifts are low noise ridges with a cold, faint rim of light. Along their crests, spindrift streams sideways during gusts, the ground itself smoking with snow.

Gloom is the palette's discipline. The sky is slate and iron, without a single warm note. A faint pallor near the horizon suggests a sun that won't break through. The snow is never pure white: it is the grey-blue of the air it falls through, brightening only up close. All of it holds back behind the words above it, as a storm seen through a cabin window should.

One life moves through the storm. We stand right among the trees. On the left a dark trunk rises steeply from the bottom corner; on the right another rises from nearer the middle and leans away, a shade darker, so the two never mirror each other. Their windward sides are plastered with soft patches of snow, and their branches reach only a little way in, so the middle stays open for the words. The trees lean slightly in the big gusts.

Every four minutes a snowy owl comes out of the white, small and faint at first, gliding on still, spread wings. It brakes with them raised and folds them as it lands on the left tree. It is drawn as the bird is: a round, neckless head, clean white plumage shaded only by the cold light, faint crescents across the breast, a black beak half buried in bristles, and lemon-yellow eyes hooded under heavy white brows, which is what makes its stare so fierce. It glares, glances aside, then lets its eyes slide to slits and dozes. Later it wakes, blinks slowly three times and turns its face away. It leaps straight back from the branch with two slow beats of its long wings and glides away into the storm until the snow swallows it.

The desktop app sets two hard limits, found on 16 November 2026. Scenes much over 110 000 characters stop moving, so the snow is thinner than it once was. Anything that repeats faster than about once a second freezes the whole scene, so every motion here, the owl's wingbeats included, is slow.
