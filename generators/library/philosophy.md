# Quiet in the Stacks

An algorithmic philosophy for the library backdrop.

A great library isn't a room with books in it. It's a wall of books with a room in front of it. Here the wall is everything: dark wooden shelving runs floor to ceiling and wall to wall, from a carved cornice at the top to a plinth at the foot, in bays split by tall uprights. Nothing else is in the way. We stand close, facing the shelves, as a reader does.

The books are shelved, not scattered. Each shelf is filled from the left as a librarian would fill it: spines mostly a hand's width or less, a few fat volumes, heights that vary but always fit under the shelf above. Now and then a run of matching volumes, a set, sits together in one colour and one height. Where a row runs out of room, the last book leans against its neighbour. Here and there a short pile lies flat, or a gap shows where a book is out. A few old volumes are chained to their shelves. The leathers are old and quiet: oxblood, bottle green, navy, tan, plum, ochre, a little worn vellum. Some spines carry faded gilt bands or a dark label.

The light is low and warm, from lamps we never see. Two pools of it, one high on the left and one on the right, with a fainter glow low in the middle, fall off into deep brown shadow at the edges and the cornice. The light is baked into every book and board when the scene is built, so the room costs almost nothing to draw. A soft glow breathes over each pool on its own slow cycle, and a few motes of dust drift up through the light.

Magic is rare and quiet. Now and then a single book somewhere on the shelves begins to glow, throwing a warm light over the books around it. As it glows hottest, a dark rune stands out on its spine in relief, lit along its upper edge and shadowed below, then sinks back as the glow fades. Each of the three books has its own rune and its own prime-length cycle, so they never fall into a rhythm and seldom overlap. The dust motes in the lamplight glimmer now and then, each on its own slow cycle. Every seventy seconds one book on the right flares and vanishes, leaving its gap empty for a while before it is back. And low on the left, in a gap on the bottom shelf, a small pair of red eyes glows in the dark and now and then blinks.

One life moves here. Every eighty seconds the Librarian, an orangutan seen only from behind, climbs down into view from above the frame, hand over hand down the front of the shelves, alternately on the left of the words and on the right. It is drawn as the animal is from behind: one great mound of long, even, rust-red hair from crown to ragged hem, the head no more than the top of it, the hair parting at the crown and flowing down over the shoulders and back. Its arms are longer than its body, and they and its short bent legs wear the same hair, hanging in locks. On the way down it swings under whichever hand is holding, tilting towards it, its legs trailing. It settles, hanging by one long arm, reaches across with the other and hooks a book out by its top. It opens the book in front of its face, so from behind we see only the covers either side of its head, and reads for a while, head tilting now and then. Then it slides the book back into its gap, looks away, and moves off along the shelf hand over hand until it leaves the frame. Its arms are posed by working out each joint from where the hands must be, so the hands really do grip the shelves. One visit's motion serves both sides: every second visit the orangutan is drawn mirrored, reaching for a twin book at the mirror-image spot.

The desktop app sets two hard limits, found in November 2026. Scenes much over 110 000 characters stop moving, and anything that repeats faster than about once a second freezes the whole scene. So every move here is slow: each hand-over-hand step takes well over a second, the glows and runes rise and fade over several seconds, and nothing flickers.
