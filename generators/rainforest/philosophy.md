# Understorey Breath

An algorithmic philosophy for the rainforest backdrop.

A rainforest isn't drawn; it's grown, then left to rot a little. Every form in this scene comes from a process, not a placement. Trunks are walked upwards through a slow noise field, so none of them is ever quite straight. Near the ground the same walk widens into buttress roots, because the width function itself swells near the soil. Branches split recursively. At each fork they turn by angles near the golden ratio, slightly disturbed, and get shorter by fixed ratios until they are twigs. The leaves cluster where the recursion ends, as they do on a real tree. Everything is grown with care and patient tuning, so nothing feels random.

Depth is atmospheric, not geometric. Each layer, from the far trunks to the near leaves, is drawn from the same algorithms, then pushed through a fog function that mixes its colour towards the humid grey-green of the air. The farthest layer is also softly blurred; the nearest is blurred differently, as a lens would. The focus falls on the middle distance, where the animals live. The fog curve was tuned until the haze felt wet enough to touch.

The forest floor is a flow field. Hundreds of fallen leaves take their angle from layered value noise, so drifts and eddies of litter form, as if water had run over the ground in the last storm. Their colour depends on depth and on a second noise field, so patches of fresh fall sit beside older, darker rot. A thinner layer of litter lies on top of the creatures that live in it, so they can vanish into it.

Light is the only fast thing up high, and it's still slow. Light shafts breathe on long, unrelated periods. Flecks of sunlight on the floor flicker at four different rates, so they never fall into a rhythm, like light through moving leaves far above. Mist is a field of soft, noise-placed bodies drifting back and forth in bands, never repeating in a way the eye can catch. Spores rise through the shafts on a flow, only visible where the light finds them.

Life uses the same idea of time. Every creature lives on its own cycle, chosen from primes so the cycles rarely line up. Some come in groups, like weaver ants in a line and fireflies flashing together. Others come alone. One cycle is a story, not a loop: a tiger stalks, freezes, pounces and misses, and the macaques scatter. It's timed so that, among all the drifting, unsynchronised motion, one moment suddenly lines up.
